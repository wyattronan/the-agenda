    About

    The theory

    During the 2026 midterm campaign, Democrats told us their agenda upon winning back power. By aggregating every winner's campaign priorities, we've determined the 30-plus most prominent agenda items for the Senate and for state legislatures, which we can reasonably understand as the agenda voters elected them to pass.

    It's not a comprehensive issue rundown. But these issues were on voters' minds.

    Using our tools, you can see which of their top priorities are likely to pass the Senate and make it through Democratic trifectas based on stated support and sponsorship, where Democrats will run into trouble implementing their agenda, and who needs (and is open to) convincing.

    You can see which states have long been implementing this popular agenda, which leaders have proven committed to pushing it through, and what the 2028 election spells for Democratic leadership and majorities.

    And you can see how exactly leading Republican voices are licking their wounds and moving forward to redefine their party, and what that all means for progress.

    Methodology: why these issues?

    The Agenda tracks what Democrats have actually promised to do with the power they won in November 2026, then asks the practical question: are there the votes?

    The Senate list

    Each U.S. Senate measure is something Democratic senators, sitting and newly elected, have committed to through bills they've written or cosponsored, votes they've taken, or 2026 campaign pledges. The list centers on the affordability fights that defined the midterms (health costs, groceries, housing, child care, taxes), plus the guardrail fights (stock trading, gerrymandering, ICE, war powers, tariffs), and the Senate-rules questions that decide whether any of it can pass. A few measures (the PRO Act, a corporate-PAC ban, term limits, paid leave, farm right-to-repair) come mostly from the incoming class, so they were researched only for senators-elect.

    The governors list

    The statehouse list comes from the same place, one level down: what Democratic governors-elect ran on in 2026 and what returning Democratic governors proposed in their 2026 State of the State addresses and budgets. That covers Medicaid, drug prices, medical debt, data centers, utility bills, housing, property and middle-class taxes, pre-K, child care, and teacher pay, plus the original rights and democracy items (abortion rights, the Popular Vote Compact, paid leave, the minimum wage, automatic voter registration).

    How positions are filled

    Only a person's own statement, cosponsorship, or recorded vote counts. Anyone without one starts as Undecided; party is never used to fill a position. The one exception is our rule for power measures: Republicans start as No on ending the filibuster, DC/PR statehood, and a gerrymandering ban (and, in state senates, the Popular Vote Compact and automatic voter registration). Green on the governors map means a state already has the policy, from published trackers. The 2028 starting ratings average every statewide result from 2024 through the 2026 midterms.

    Getting Stuff Done scores

    Every leader is graded issue by issue on a ladder, at the highest rung their record reaches: Deliver (signed, enacted, or a law they wrote that passed), Spend (vetoes, lawsuits, forced votes, whip efforts, money, executive orders), Commit (recorded votes, lead sponsorship, pledges), or Talk (statements, cosponsorships), worth 100%, 50%, 25%, and 10% of an issue's credit; a lean earns half. That ladder average is scaled so the best record in each group sets 90, leaving room to improve (no one can reach 100), and is 75% of the score. The other 25% is Steward: for governors, their state's average rank on four outside report cards (the Commonwealth Fund's 2025 health system scorecard, MERIC's Q3 2026 cost-of-living index, the 2026 KIDS COUNT Data Book, and the 2024 Cost of Voting Index); for members of Congress, their recorded votes defending laws already on the books. A governor's priority that's already law at home counts only if they signed it or pushed it nationally during the 2026 campaign or since. Presidential hopefuls are graded on both agendas at once; those without a current office are scored on the ladder alone.

    Supporters, champions, and top champions. Anyone on record in favor of a measure is a supporter. A champion moved it themselves, at the Commit rung or higher: a senator who's the lead sponsor, or a leader who pledged it, put out a plan, or proposed it. Votes, cosponsorships, and statements count as support, not championing. A top champion went further, to Spend or Deliver: signed it, forced a vote, sued, vetoed, or put money behind it.
