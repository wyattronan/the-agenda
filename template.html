      About

      The theory

      During the 2026 midterm campaign, Democrats told us their agenda upon winning back power. By aggregating every winner's campaign priorities, we've determined the 30-plus most prominent agenda items for the Senate and for state legislatures, which we can reasonably understand as the agenda voters elected them to pass.

      It's not a comprehensive issue rundown. But these issues were on voters' minds.

      Using our tools, you can see which of their top priorities are likely to pass the Senate and make it through Democratic trifectas based on stated support and sponsorship, where Democrats will run into trouble implementing their agenda, and who needs (and is open to) convincing.

      You can see which states have long been implementing this popular agenda, which leaders have proven committed to pushing it through, and what the 2028 election spells for Democratic leadership and majorities.

      And you can see how exactly leading Republican voices are licking their wounds and moving forward to redefine their party, and what that all means for progress.

      Methodology: why these issues?

      The Agenda tracks what Democrats have actually promised to do with the power they won in November 2026, then asks the practical question: are there the votes?

      The Senate list

      The Senate list is the 30 measures the most 2026 Democratic Senate winners campaigned on. In November 2026 we went through what all 22 of them ran on (campaign issue pages, ads, debates, questionnaires and plans, each with a source) and counted how many winners ran on each measure, once per person. Legislative records counted only when a campaign featured them. Broad themes with no single bill behind them (farmers, veterans, "anti-corruption") were left out, and two items that are already law, the ROAD to Housing Act and its ban on big investors buying homes, were dropped. Ties at the cutoff went to measures already researched.

      Hot buttons. A few measures fall outside that top 30 but stay on the site because they're defining fights: ending the filibuster, the Schumer leadership race, DC/PR statehood, Medicare for All, national paid leave, and a ban on partisan gerrymandering. They carry a Hot button tag; hover it to see how many winners ran on each.

      The governors list

      The statehouse list works the same way, one level down: the 30 policies the most of the 24 Democratic governor winners of 2026 campaigned on, from the same census. Some older items were widened to match how candidates talked about them (for example, zoning reform became "build more homes," and the drug price board became "lower drug prices"), and a few were folded together (rate freezes and price caps into utility bills, corporate landlords into renter protections). The hot buttons here are the Popular Vote Compact, paid family leave, a millionaire's tax, keeping a federalized National Guard out, and fully funding public schools instead of vouchers.

      How positions are filled

      Only a person's own statement, cosponsorship, or recorded vote counts. Anyone without one starts as Undecided; party is never used to fill a position. The one exception is our rule for power measures: Republicans start as No on ending the filibuster, DC/PR statehood, and a gerrymandering ban (and, in state senates, the Popular Vote Compact and automatic voter registration). Green on the governors map means a state already has the policy, from published trackers. The 2028 starting ratings average every statewide result from 2024 through the 2026 midterms.

      Getting Stuff Done scores

      Every leader is graded issue by issue on the Impact Meter, at the highest level their record reaches: Deliver (signed, enacted, or a law they wrote that passed), Spend (vetoes, lawsuits, forced votes, whip efforts, executive orders, and money: public funds, or spending by a PAC or issue group they founded, lead or control, on the measure itself or on candidates' ads built around it), Commit (recorded votes, lead sponsorship, pledges), or Talk (statements, cosponsorships), worth 100%, 50%, 25%, and 10% of an issue's credit; a lean earns half. That meter average is scaled so the best record in each group sets 90, leaving room to improve (no one can reach 100), and is 75% of the score. The other 25% is Steward: for governors, their state's average rank on four outside report cards (the Commonwealth Fund's 2025 health system scorecard, MERIC's Q3 2026 cost-of-living index, the 2026 KIDS COUNT Data Book, and the 2024 Cost of Voting Index); for members of Congress, their recorded votes defending laws already on the books. A governor's priority that's already law at home counts only if they signed it or pushed it nationally during the 2026 campaign or since. Presidential hopefuls are graded on both agendas at once; those without a current office are scored on the Impact Meter alone.

      Supporters, champions, and top champions. Anyone on record in favor of a measure is a supporter. A champion moved it themselves, at the Commit level or higher: a senator who's the lead sponsor, or a leader who pledged it, put out a plan, or proposed it. Votes, cosponsorships, and statements count as support, not championing. A top champion went further, to Spend or Deliver: signed it, forced a vote, sued, vetoed, or put money behind it.
